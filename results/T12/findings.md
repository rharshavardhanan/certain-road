# T12 — findings

Authored companion to `allocation.md`, which is generated by
`scripts/exp_allocation.py` and overwritten on every run.

## The optimiser earns its place only when priority and cost decouple

| budget | regime | optimal | greedy | gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | uniform traffic | 712 | 712 | **0.0%** |
| 20% | uniform traffic | 1,399 | 1,398 | 0.1% |
| 10% | varying traffic | 2,525 | 2,425 | **+4.1%** |
| 20% | varying traffic | 3,871 | 3,743 | +3.4% |
| 30% | varying traffic | 4,858 | 4,729 | +2.7% |
| 50% | varying traffic | 6,256 | 6,150 | +1.7% |

**Under uniform traffic the exact optimiser is worth nothing.** Cost rises
monotonically with damage and priority *is* damage, so priority-per-rupee is
nearly monotone and repairing the worst segment that still fits is already
optimal. The knapsack structure the DP exists to handle never arises.

This is a property of the cost model, not of the solver, and it was worth
finding: had only this regime been run, the honest conclusion would have been
"do not bother optimising".

**Traffic weight is what makes the decision hard.** Once a busy road with
moderate damage can outrank a quiet road with severe damage while costing less
to repair, priority and cost stop moving together and the optimiser separates —
by 4.1% at the tightest budget, shrinking to 1.7% as the budget grows and the
problem stops binding.

**The gain is largest exactly where it matters.** A 4% improvement at a 10%
budget is worth more than a 4% improvement at 50%, because the tight budget is
the case where something goes unrepaired.

## Greedy beats the optimiser on the metric that may matter more

| budget | regime | optimal worst-20 | greedy worst-20 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | varying | 69.0% | **72.5%** |
| 20% | varying | 95.4% | **100.0%** |
| 30% | varying | 99.7% | **100.0%** |

The optimiser maximises **total** benefit, which it can raise by repairing many
moderately-damaged busy segments instead of a few severe ones. Greedy-worst-first
targets severity directly and covers the worst 20 more completely at every tight
budget.

**These two metrics encode different objectives, and neither is simply correct.**
Maximum total benefit is the utilitarian answer. "No severely damaged road is
left unrepaired" is usually the safety and political requirement. A dashboard
that reports only the first would recommend a plan a road authority could not
defend.

Both policies remain far ahead of random, which reaches only 9.6% of the worst
20 at a 10% budget against greedy's 72.5% — so the ranking that matters most is
*any principled policy* over none.

## Caveats

- `detector_recall = 0.75` is a placeholder. The real figure is the operating
  point T10 certifies, and this experiment should be re-run once that exists.
- Damage is `Beta(2,5) x 100` — right-skewed, so most segments are fair and a
  minority are bad. A uniform distribution would flatten the differences between
  all three policies.
- The network is synthetic. This measures *policy* behaviour under a stated cost
  model, not any real road network.

## A solver bug this experiment exposed

Varying traffic pushed priorities to ~1000, so at `PRIORITY_QUANTUM = 0.01` the
DP table reached ~20M entries and the run stalled. `allocate_optimal` now sizes
its quantum to keep the table under `MAX_TABLE_ENTRIES`, giving 0.128 s per
trial. The resulting error is at most one quantum per chosen segment — far below
the precision of the vision-estimated PCI feeding it.
